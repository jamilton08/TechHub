Smart Moves: build a game AI

Every enemy that ever chased you, every ghost in Pac-Man, every guard that shouted "Who's there?" was a few lines of code answering two questions, over and over: what can I sense? and where should I go? You'll start where AI started, then build a brain for a small robot. It will learn to see danger as a number, follow if / elif / else rules, score every possible move and pick the best one, and a castle guard will switch between patrol, chase, search and flee. At the end, you decide how "intelligent" it really is.

    Where game AI comes from

    "Artificial intelligence" is older than video games. For 75 years people have argued about what it means for a machine to think, and game makers have borrowed every idea along the way. Here is the short version.

      Every game AI is one loop

      Whether it's a Pac-Man ghost or a boss fight, every turn (or every frame) the game runs the same three steps for each character. The only part that makes one character "smarter" than another is the middle one.

      1. SENSEs = sense(world)The engine builds a dict of everything the robot is allowed to know: its HP, where the goal is, what's in each square next to it.
      2. THINKmove = think(s)Your code. It reads s and returns one word: "up", "down", "left", "right" or "stay".
      3. ACTstep(world, move)The engine moves the robot, applies lava and spikes, moves the enemies, then checks for the flag.
      ↺ next turn

      The robot never sees the map. It only sees s. That's true of real game AI too: it only "knows" what the programmer lets it sense.

        Sense: give danger a number
        
          
        A robot can't be scared of lava. It can only compare numbers. So the first job of a game-AI designer is to turn every object in the world into a score: how bad is it to stand here? Careful from Stop 1 used exactly that: "if the danger is 50 or more, take the safest step instead."

                Stop 4 of 6 · 20 points
                
          
                Score every move
                
          
                Rules only cover the situations someone thought of. The other big idea in game AI flips it around: give every possible move a score, then pick the highest. Closer to the goal? Points. Dangerous? Lose points. The Sims, strategy games and chess engines all work this way. It's called utility AI.

                Stop 5 of 6 · 20 points
                
          
                States: the castle guard
                
          
                A guard should act differently depending on what's going on: walk its rounds, chase you, hunt for you after losing you, or run when it's hurt. Each of those is a state, and the guard is always in exactly one. Every turn, one small function decides whether to switch. This is a finite state machine, and it runs a huge share of the enemies in games.

                  Night Watch: sneak past your guard
                  
          
                  You're the hero. Every step you take is one turn, then the guard runs your next_state(g) and moves. Walk into the guard to bonk it (−1 HP). It sees in straight lines, up to 5 squares, and not through walls (the tinted squares are what it can see right now). Break its line of sight by ducking around a corner.

                      Stop 6 of 6 · 20 points
                      
          
                      Whose idea was it?
                      
          
                      You built something that dodges lava, finds its way around walls it has never seen, and a guard that hunts you. Nobody would call it a person. So is it intelligent? Is anything it does original? These questions don't have one-word answers, and people who build AI for a living still argue about them. Think each one through.

                      See an example (a museum guard dog)
            
                      
              
                      Senses
                      How far the player is, whether it can smell them (within 3 squares, even through walls), its own energy, whether its owner is nearby.
                      
              
                      States
                      Sleep → Sniff when it smells the player → Chase when it sees them → Bark when the player is cornered → back to Sleep when its energy is low.
                      
              
                      Scores
                      +20 for every step closer to the smell, −50 for squares with water, +30 near its owner, −10 for squares it already sniffed.
                      
              
                      Fair
                      It barks once before it chases, so the player has a warning. It can't open doors. It gets tired after 10 turns of chasing.
