Packet Run: get it there, keep it secret

Everything you do online travels as packets: small envelopes that hop from device to device until they reach the right address. You'll build a network, teach its routers where to send things, and send packets through it while a hacker listens in. Your job: get every packet to the right place, and keep the secret ones secret.

    Stop 1 of 6 · 10 points

    Anatomy of a packet

    Maya is at the library, logging in to the school's grades website. Her login travels as a packet. Build it, send it, and watch every hop. Someone else on the library Wi-Fi is watching too.

    Header

    From, To, Port, TTL. Every device along the way reads it, so it can't be hidden.

    Payload

    The message itself. This is the part encryption can scramble.

    Hops

    Each router reads the To address and passes the packet one step closer. Each router it passes uses up 1 TTL.

    Wi-Fi

    Radio waves. On open Wi-Fi (no password, like most libraries and airports) any device in range can pick up every packet.

    Try it

      Stop 2 of 6 · 15 points

      Build a network

      Drag pieces onto the board, then use the Cable tool to connect them. A switch or a Wi-Fi access point, plus everything plugged into it, is one network. Its router is the network's way out. Devices get their addresses automatically (that's DHCP): everyone in Network A gets 10.0.1.something, Network B gets 10.0.2.something, and so on.

      Your network needs

        Address book (handed out by DHCP)

          Stop 3 of 6 · 15 points

          Teach the routers

          A router only knows the networks plugged straight into it. For every other network it needs a route: "packets for Network C go to Router 2." Drag network cards onto each router's arrows. Anything else is the default route: the router's best guess, usually toward the internet.

          To earn the points

            Test every path

            Sends a test packet from each network to every other one. Click a result to watch that packet.

            Real routers fill in these tables by themselves, using routing protocols (OSPF inside a school, BGP between internet providers). The Internet piece here already does that. Doing it by hand is how you learn what they're doing.

            Stop 4 of 6 · 20 points

            Send packets

            Now play your network. Build a packet (drag an address, a service and maybe the lock), send it, and see where it ends up and who saw it. Try to break things on purpose: that's how you'll recognize the problems later.
