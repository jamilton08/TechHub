The Seized Drive: a digital forensics case

You caught the hacker. Now comes the real work. He stole a file of patient records from the Northside Clinic, and it's your job to prove what he took, when it left the network, and where it went — without touching his systems and without ever revealing the victims' data. This is digital forensics: the careful, legal side of security. You have his seized laptop and a signed warrant. Work the case one step at a time.

This is not "hack him back." Breaking into someone's computer is a crime even when that someone is a thief. Real investigators never do it. Everything here goes through a warrant, a forensic copy, and the courts — which is exactly why the evidence holds up.

    Chain of custody: handle the drive right

    Evidence is only worth something if you can prove nobody changed it. Before you read a single file, you have to protect the drive and make a copy you can prove is identical. Get this wrong and everything you find later can be thrown out of court.

        Step 3 of 6 · 15 points

        Verify the files with hashes

        A hash is a short fingerprint of a file: the same file always gives the same hash, and changing even one byte changes it completely. The clinic recorded a hash for every important file before the break-in. Compare them with the drive now to catch what the intruder changed — and what he left behind.

          Step 4 of 6 · 20 points

          Trace where the data went

          The log showed 48 MB leaving for an outside address. Some of these addresses are private — they only exist inside a local network and can't be reached across the internet — and some are public. Sort them out, look up who owns the public ones, and find the drop server. Then do the one legal thing you're allowed to do next.

          Public or private?

          Private ranges (10.x, 172.16–31.x, 192.168.x) live inside a network. A server out on the internet needs a public address.
